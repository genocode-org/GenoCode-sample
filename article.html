A small garden in spring

You don't need much room for a garden. A window box, a few pots by the door or one corner of a yard is enough to watch a whole season go by.

Spring starts slowly. The first warm week wakes the bulbs you planted in autumn, and before long the buds are fat and green. Then, one sunny morning, they open. Most flowers open in the morning and close a little in the evening, which is why a garden looks different at breakfast and at dinner.

Choosing what to plant

Plant what grows well where you live: your neighbours' gardens are the best guide. Flowers that come back every year, such as daisies, save work. Annuals give the brightest colour but need planting again each spring. Mix a few tall plants at the back with low ones in front, so nothing hides.

Water in the morning rather than at night, and water the soil, not the leaves. A layer of mulch keeps the ground damp for longer and the weeds down.

Visitors

A garden in flower soon has guests. Bees and hoverflies come for the nectar, ladybirds come for the aphids, and sooner or later a caterpillar turns up to eat a leaf or two.

Before you pick it off, remember what it's going to become. Most caterpillars do little harm, and a few chewed leaves are a fair price for butterflies later in summer. If one plant is being eaten bare, move the caterpillar to a weed or a wilder corner instead.

Leave a little mess for the visitors: a pile of sticks, a patch of long grass, a shallow dish of water with a stone to stand on. The tidier the garden, the fewer guests it has.

Looking ahead

By the end of spring the garden has found its shape. Write down what did well and what didn't. Next year's garden starts with this year's notes.
